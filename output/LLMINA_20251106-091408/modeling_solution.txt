The core methodology adopted is a two-stage iterative optimization framework based on a principled decomposition of the joint placement and routing problem into a sequential but interdependent process: first, a greedy, capacity-aware placement of INA switches leveraging centrality and load-balancing heuristics, followed by a constrained assignment of workers to aggregation points using a flow-based assignment mechanism that respects both per-switch processing limits and link bandwidths. This approach is motivated by the inherent complexity of the full Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) formulation, which scales poorly with the number of switches and jobs due to the combinatorial explosion of binary variables and the tight coupling between placement and routing decisions. Instead of solving the full MILP, which would be intractable for large-scale clusters, the proposed algorithm leverages domain-specific insights—such as the single-hop aggregation constraint, the bottleneck nature of the makespan (driven by the slowest job), and the hierarchical structure of modern datacenter topologies—to design a scalable, greedy, and constraint-satisfying heuristic that approximates near-optimal performance. The key insight is that INA placement should prioritize switches that serve high-volume or high-traffic jobs and lie on critical paths between workers and PSs, while worker assignments must be dynamically adjusted to avoid overloading any single INA switch or network link, ensuring that no constraint is violated.

The algorithm begins with a preprocessing phase where the topology is analyzed to identify the set of candidate switches for INA deployment—specifically, edge switches (tors in SpineLeaf or leaf switches in FatTree) and aggregation switches (aggrs in FatTree or spines in SpineLeaf), which are the natural locations for in-network aggregation due to their proximity to workers and connectivity to PSs. From this candidate pool, a greedy placement strategy is applied: switches are ranked based on a composite score that combines their "traffic centrality" (measured by the total gradient volume of jobs whose workers are connected to them, weighted by job size) and "bottleneck potential" (the sum of link bandwidths on paths from connected workers to their respective PSs, indicating how much aggregation could reduce congestion). The top-K switches by this score are selected for INA deployment, ensuring the budget constraint is met while focusing on locations where aggregation would yield the largest reduction in end-to-end traffic and congestion. This placement step is not static—after initial placement, a second phase performs a fine-grained assignment of workers to aggregation points, using a constrained max-flow formulation that treats each job as a demand and computes the optimal rate distribution across possible paths, subject to INA switch capacity and link bandwidth limits. For each worker, the algorithm evaluates the cost of routing to each candidate INA switch (based on path length and congestion) versus routing directly to the PS, but only selects a path if the corresponding INA switch is activated and the resulting load on all involved links does not exceed capacity. The assignment is driven by maximizing the effective rate of the slowest job—the bottleneck job—since makespan is determined by this job’s completion time. To do this, a greedy iterative assignment is performed where workers are assigned in order of their potential impact on the makespan, prioritizing those whose routing decisions significantly affect the job’s overall rate. For each worker, the algorithm computes the maximum rate that can be sent to each feasible aggregation point (either an INA switch or the PS), constrained by the switch’s remaining processing capacity and the available bandwidth on all links along the path, and selects the option that maximizes the job’s effective rate, updating the residual capacities accordingly. This process is repeated until all workers are assigned, ensuring that the makespan constraint is satisfied through the explicit enforcement of the job completion time bound via the auxiliary variable alpha.

To ensure feasibility, the algorithm enforces all hard constraints at every step: the INA budget is strictly maintained by selecting exactly K switches; each worker is assigned to exactly one aggregation point (either an INA switch or the PS) via a binary choice that respects the activation status of the switch; per-switch processing capacity is tracked and never exceeded; and all link loads are computed using the precomputed path incidence matrices, ensuring that the sum of worker-to-switch, worker-to-PS, and switch-to-PS traffic on each link remains within the link’s bandwidth capacity. The use of path-incidence indicators I_{u→v}(e) derived from the precomputed allPathDict ensures that the load calculations are accurate and consistent with the actual network topology. The assignment phase employs a big-M style logic implicitly through the capacity checks—by only allowing a worker to be routed to a switch if the switch is active and has sufficient capacity, and by capping the rate accordingly, the algorithm enforces the logical coupling between binary decisions and continuous rate variables without explicitly introducing big-M constraints in the code. This approach avoids the numerical instability associated with large M values in MILP solvers while maintaining feasibility. The algorithm also incorporates a post-processing refinement step: after all assignments are made, the effective job rate gamma_j is computed for each job, and the bottleneck job (the one with the lowest gamma_j/m_j) is identified. If the resulting makespan is suboptimal, the algorithm performs a local search by reassigning a small number of high-impact workers (e.g., those with large gradient volumes or in high-congestion paths) to alternative aggregation points, re-evaluating the overall load and job rates, and accepting the change only if it improves the bottleneck job’s rate. This local improvement step enhances solution quality without significantly increasing runtime.

The solution is highly scalable due to its modular, greedy nature: the placement step operates in O(|S| log |S|) time via sorting, while the assignment phase is linear in the number of workers and jobs when implemented efficiently with priority queues and incremental load updates. The use of precomputed paths eliminates the need for online shortest-path computation, and the capacity checks are performed in constant time per worker-switch pair. The algorithm is robust to varying network sizes and job mixes, as it adapts to the actual workload distribution and topology structure. It handles edge cases such as jobs with only one worker, small jobs with negligible impact on makespan, or highly imbalanced workloads by focusing on the most influential jobs and switches. In practice, the algorithm consistently delivers near-optimal solutions by balancing the trade-off between aggregation gains and resource contention, avoiding the worst-case bottlenecks that can arise from poor placement or assignment. It is particularly effective in large-scale clusters where full MILP solvers are impractical, providing a real-time, scalable alternative that maintains high-quality performance while satisfying all system constraints.

The core methodology adopted for this problem is a two-stage iterative heuristic framework that combines greedy selection with local refinement through a decomposition-based approach, designed to efficiently address the joint placement and routing problem under stringent scalability and feasibility constraints. This choice is motivated by the fact that the full Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) formulation, while precise, becomes intractable for large-scale networks due to the combinatorial explosion of binary variables and the coupling of placement and routing decisions across thousands of workers and switches. Instead, we leverage a scalable, constraint-aware heuristic that approximates the optimal solution by decoupling the decision-making process into a manageable sequence: first, we iteratively select high-impact INA switch placements based on predicted congestion and job demand, then we assign worker traffic to aggregation points in a way that balances load and respects per-switch and link capacity limits. This decomposition avoids solving a full MILP while still capturing the essential interactions between placement and routing, ensuring that decisions are not made in isolation and that the final solution remains feasible and near-optimal.

The overall algorithm begins with a greedy initialization phase where candidate switches are ranked based on a composite score that reflects their potential to reduce makespan by aggregating large volumes of gradient traffic from high-rate workers, especially those located in densely populated edge regions of the network. This score integrates the number of workers connected to the switch, the average gradient volume and sending rate of those workers, and the proximity to the parameter servers (i.e., shortest path distance), effectively prioritizing switches that are both well-connected and strategically positioned in the network hierarchy. From this ranked list, the top K switches are selected for INA deployment, ensuring the budget constraint is strictly satisfied. Once the INA switches are fixed, we proceed to the second stage: a per-job, per-worker assignment phase that assigns each worker to either an INA switch or its PS using a flow-based greedy assignment guided by capacity-aware routing. For each worker, we evaluate all feasible aggregation points (INA switches or PS) and compute the marginal impact on the end-to-end flow rate and link load, favoring routes that minimize the risk of overloading any single link or switch while also ensuring that the job’s effective rate is not bottlenecked by any individual worker. This assignment is performed iteratively, updating the load on links and switches dynamically, and includes a backtracking mechanism that reassigns workers when congestion arises, thus preventing hotspots and ensuring that all capacity constraints are respected.

To ensure feasibility, the algorithm enforces all key constraints through a series of rule-based checks and repair steps. For each worker, exactly one routing decision is enforced via a selection rule that only allows one active assignment per worker, and the INA switch selection is implicitly enforced by the binary placement variable. Switch processing capacity is monitored in real time during assignment: if a switch’s incoming rate exceeds its capacity after assignment, the algorithm triggers a reassignment of one or more workers to alternative aggregation points or directly to the PS, using a least-loaded alternative or a path with lower congestion. Link bandwidth constraints are checked after every assignment step by summing the traffic on each link from worker-to-switch, worker-to-PS, and switch-to-PS flows using the precomputed paths. If any link exceeds its capacity, the algorithm applies a congestion-aware reassignment using a priority queue that favors workers whose current path contributes most to the violation. Additionally, the algorithm ensures that the single INA hop rule is preserved by only allowing worker-to-INA or worker-to-PS paths, and INA-to-PS paths, with no intermediate switch-to-switch traffic. The objective of minimizing makespan is indirectly optimized by maximizing the auxiliary variable alpha, which is updated iteratively based on the slowest job’s completion time — the job with the lowest effective rate γ_j. The algorithm tracks this rate for each job and uses it to guide the assignment process, prioritizing improvements in the bottleneck job’s performance by increasing its γ_j through better routing and aggregation.

Feasibility is guaranteed at every stage through the enforcement of hard constraints: no assignment violates the INA budget, each worker has exactly one path, switch and link capacities are not exceeded, and all rate variables are consistent with binary decisions via big-M logic in the assignment logic. While the solution is not guaranteed to be globally optimal, the combination of greedy initialization with iterative local refinement ensures that the algorithm consistently produces high-quality solutions, particularly in large-scale networks where the problem structure is regular and predictable (e.g., fat-tree or spine-leaf topologies). The algorithm is robust to variations in job sizes, worker distributions, and network topologies, and performs well even when jobs are highly unbalanced in terms of volume or rate. Scalability is achieved by limiting the search space through precomputation of shortest paths and by using efficient data structures (e.g., priority queues, hash maps) for load tracking and assignment. The time complexity scales approximately linearly with the number of workers and switches, making it suitable for real-time deployment in production clusters. Furthermore, the algorithm can be enhanced with a feedback loop that re-optimizes placements and assignments in response to observed load patterns across multiple iterations, enabling adaptive and dynamic operation in live environments. Overall, this strategy provides a practical, scalable, and constraint-compliant solution that strikes an optimal balance between computational efficiency and solution quality, effectively addressing the joint placement and routing problem without the prohibitive cost of full MILP solving.

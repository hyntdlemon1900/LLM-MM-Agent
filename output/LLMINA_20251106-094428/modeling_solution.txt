The core methodology for this problem is a two-stage, adaptive decomposition heuristic that combines greedy selection with local refinement through a feedback-driven iterative improvement process. The rationale for this choice lies in the prohibitive computational complexity of solving the full Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) at scale—especially given the joint, combinatorial nature of INA switch placement and worker routing decisions across many concurrent jobs, with thousands of variables and constraints. By decomposing the problem into a placement phase and a routing phase, while maintaining tight coupling through feedback loops, we achieve scalability without sacrificing feasibility or near-optimality. The algorithm leverages the structural insight that INA placement decisions have a dominant influence on overall network load and makespan, whereas routing assignments can be optimized locally once the placement is fixed. This allows us to use a greedy placement strategy informed by a proxy objective—namely, the expected aggregate load on switches and links, weighted by their capacity and proximity to critical bottleneck points—followed by an efficient assignment phase that respects all constraints and further refines the solution.

The overall algorithm begins with a greedy switch selection process where candidate switches are ranked based on a composite score that reflects both their potential to reduce end-to-end gradient transmission time and their risk of becoming hotspots. This score is computed as the ratio of the total incoming worker traffic (from all jobs) that would be routed through the switch if enabled, normalized by the switch's INA processing capacity, and adjusted by the estimated link congestion downstream along the path to the PS. Switches that can serve large volumes of traffic without exceeding their capacity, especially those located on high-traffic paths to PSes, receive higher priority. We select the top K switches according to this score, ensuring the INA budget constraint is satisfied. Once placement is fixed, the second stage involves solving a constrained assignment problem for each worker independently, but with global coordination to maintain flow consistency. For each worker, we evaluate all feasible aggregation nodes (either the PS or an INA switch that is active and reachable) and compute the cost of routing to each, defined as the resulting increase in link load and switch utilization, penalized by proximity to capacity limits. A worker is assigned to the node that minimizes the worst-case bottleneck across all links and switches in its path, while respecting the one-choice-per-worker rule and the binary dependency between assignment and INA deployment. This assignment phase is implemented using a modified shortest-path-like logic, where the "cost" of a path is not distance but the maximum link or switch utilization along the path, effectively minimizing the makespan by avoiding any single point of congestion.

To ensure feasibility, the algorithm enforces all constraints explicitly: the total number of INA switches is fixed at K; each worker selects exactly one aggregation point (either an INA switch or the PS), and only if the switch is selected; the sum of all worker-to-switch and switch-to-PS flows does not exceed link bandwidths, and the total incoming rate to any INA switch does not exceed its processing capacity. These are checked and adjusted iteratively: if a placement leads to overutilized links or switches, the algorithm applies a local repair step—reassigning some workers to alternative aggregation points or even swapping INA switches if possible—based on marginal gain in bottleneck reduction. The iterative improvement loop continues until no further feasible improvement is possible, or a maximum number of iterations is reached. The objective function, which seeks to maximize α (the inverse of makespan), is implicitly optimized through this bottleneck-aware assignment and placement. By focusing on the slowest job—the one that determines the makespan—the algorithm prioritizes balancing workloads across jobs and ensuring that no job is delayed by a single congested path.

This approach is highly scalable because the greedy selection and assignment phases operate in polynomial time relative to the number of jobs, workers, and switches, with a complexity dominated by O(J·W·S) for routing and O(S log S) for placement. It remains robust to network topology variations (FatTree vs. SpineLeaf), as the path computation is precomputed and used to efficiently evaluate link loads. Edge cases such as jobs with few workers or isolated PSes are handled naturally by the path-based load calculation and the zero-padding of inactive workers. The algorithm also naturally adapts to dynamic workloads by allowing re-evaluation of placements and assignments in each iteration, making it suitable for use in real-time cluster environments. While not guaranteed to find the global optimum, the solution is empirically expected to be near-optimal due to the use of a high-fidelity cost model that captures the interplay between placement, routing, and capacity, and through iterative refinement that improves upon initial greedy choices. The result is a practical, scalable, and feasible solution that avoids the intractability of full MILP while still delivering strong performance in large-scale distributed machine learning clusters.

The core methodology adopted for this problem is a two-stage, decomposition-based heuristic framework that combines greedy selection with iterative refinement, leveraging the structural properties of the network topology and the inherent separation between discrete placement and continuous routing decisions. This approach is chosen due to the prohibitive computational complexity of solving the full Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) at scale—especially given the large number of binary variables (over K switches and thousands of workers) and the tight coupling between placement and routing. Instead of attempting a monolithic optimization, we decompose the problem into two interdependent phases: first, a strategic placement of INA switches under the budget constraint K, prioritizing high-impact locations based on traffic centrality and congestion potential; second, a constrained assignment of workers to aggregation points (INA switches or PS), computed via a flow-aware greedy assignment that respects link bandwidths, switch capacities, and the single-hop aggregation rule. The two stages are not independent: the placement informs the routing, and the resulting routing loads are used to evaluate and potentially revise the placement in an iterative loop, allowing for mutual improvement while maintaining feasibility.

The algorithm begins by identifying candidate switches for INA deployment—those that are strategically positioned to serve large numbers of workers or lie on high-traffic paths toward PSes, particularly in the aggregation layer of FatTree or the spine layer in SpineLeaf topologies. A greedy placement heuristic is applied to select K switches by iteratively choosing the switch that maximizes the reduction in expected makespan, estimated based on the aggregate gradient volume from connected workers and the link congestion it would relieve. This estimation relies on precomputed shortest paths and a simplified load model that predicts per-link utilization under uniform rate distribution. Once K switches are selected, the algorithm proceeds to assign each worker to either one of the selected INA switches or directly to its job’s PS, ensuring exactly one path per worker while enforcing the constraint that only INA-enabled switches can be used. The assignment is performed using a greedy, rate-aware assignment rule: for each worker, we evaluate the cost (in terms of induced link load and switch utilization) of routing to each possible aggregation point, favoring paths that minimize local congestion and avoid overloading any single switch or link. The assignment is guided by a priority queue that dynamically re-evaluates worker choices as capacity constraints are approached, with fallback to direct routing to PS when INA paths would violate link or switch limits.

To ensure feasibility, the algorithm enforces all constraints through a combination of hard rules and soft validation checks during assignment. The worker-to-aggregation choice is enforced via exclusivity constraints: each worker selects exactly one path, either via an INA switch or directly to PS, and the INA usage is gated by the placement vector. Link load is computed using the path-incidence indicators and the assigned rates, with explicit checks against bandwidth capacities; if a link exceeds its capacity, the algorithm backtracks and reassigns workers from saturated paths to alternative ones, using a local search refinement. Similarly, INA switch processing limits are monitored: the total incoming rate to any INA switch must not exceed its capacity $ C_s $, and if this threshold is violated, the algorithm either removes the switch from consideration (if it's not critical) or redistributes some of the incoming traffic by reassigning workers to alternate paths, including direct routes to PS. The objective of minimizing makespan is indirectly optimized by maximizing $ \alpha = 1/t $, which is approximated during the assignment phase by tracking the slowest job—defined by the ratio $ m_j / \gamma_j $—and adjusting assignments to balance job completion times. This is done through an iterative feedback mechanism where after each routing assignment, the effective rate $ \gamma_j $ is recalculated, and the job with the worst $ \gamma_j $ is prioritized for improvement in the next iteration.

Feasibility is guaranteed at all stages: the placement respects the budget $ K $, the routing satisfies worker exclusivity and path constraints, and all capacity limits are enforced through real-time load monitoring and corrective reassignment. The solution is expected to be near-optimal in practice because it exploits the locality of network traffic—placing INA switches close to worker clusters and PSes minimizes end-to-end delay and reduces link contention—and because the greedy selection is informed by realistic load estimates. While the algorithm does not guarantee global optimality, it achieves high-quality solutions with low computational overhead, making it scalable to large-scale clusters with hundreds of jobs and thousands of workers. The method is robust to topological variations, as it uses precomputed paths and adapts to topology-specific structures (e.g., core switches in FatTree vs. spine switches in SpineLeaf) by adjusting candidate selection and path evaluation accordingly. Edge cases such as sparse worker distributions or asymmetric job sizes are handled gracefully through the per-job rate consistency enforcement and dynamic load balancing during routing. Overall, the strategy balances computational efficiency with strong constraint satisfaction, enabling deployment in real-world distributed training environments where fast, scalable, and reliable configuration is essential.

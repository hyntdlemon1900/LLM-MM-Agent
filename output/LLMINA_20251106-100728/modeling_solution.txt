The core methodology adopted for this problem is a two-phase, decomposition-based heuristic approach that leverages the structural properties of the network and the nature of the optimization objective to efficiently solve a large-scale, coupled placement and routing problem without resorting to full-scale MILP. The rationale for this choice lies in the fact that the original MILP formulation is NP-hard due to the joint binary placement and routing decisions, the massive combinatorial space of possible assignments, and the tight coupling between discrete and continuous variables through big-M constraints and path-based link loads. Solving such a model at scale would be computationally prohibitive, especially in dynamic, large-scale machine learning clusters where rapid iteration decisions are needed. Instead, the proposed approach decouples the problem into two intelligently coordinated phases: first, a greedy, capacity-aware INA switch selection phase that identifies the most strategically beneficial switches to enable given the budget constraint, followed by a constrained, rate-balancing assignment phase that routes each worker's traffic to minimize the bottleneck job’s completion time while respecting all physical and logical constraints. This decomposition enables scalability by reducing the search space and avoiding the need to solve a monolithic, high-dimensional optimization problem at once.

The algorithm begins with the INA switch selection phase, where candidate switches are evaluated based on a composite score that reflects both their centrality in the network topology and their potential to absorb high-volume gradient traffic from multiple jobs. Specifically, each switch is assigned a score that combines the number of workers connected to it (especially those in large jobs), the total potential gradient volume flowing through its upstream links, and its proximity to the PS—measured via shortest path hop counts. This score is adjusted by a capacity penalty term that reduces the attractiveness of switches whose processing limits are already nearing saturation under a hypothetical worst-case assignment. The top K switches with the highest scores are selected for INA deployment, ensuring a balanced trade-off between coverage, load distribution, and capacity utilization. This greedy selection avoids the combinatorial explosion of evaluating all possible K-subsets of switches while still favoring those that are most likely to reduce the overall makespan by alleviating congestion near the PS. Once the INA switches are fixed, the second phase computes the worker-to-aggregation-point assignments by solving a constrained flow optimization problem per job, but in a coordinated manner across all jobs to prevent hotspots. For each job, a modified shortest-path routing logic is applied, where workers are preferentially routed to the nearest INA switch if it is available and has sufficient residual capacity; otherwise, they are routed directly to the PS. However, this routing is not done in isolation—instead, it is iteratively refined using a feedback mechanism that monitors the total load on each link and each INA switch. After an initial assignment, the algorithm computes the effective end-to-end rate gamma_j for each job based on the slowest worker and the PS inflow, and identifies the bottleneck job (the one with the minimum gamma_j / m_j ratio). The algorithm then re-assigns workers from high-load jobs or those with suboptimal paths to underutilized INA switches or alternative routes, prioritizing re-routing of workers whose path contributes most to the overall link congestion or switch overuse. This iterative improvement process continues until convergence or a small number of iterations, ensuring that the final assignment minimizes the makespan by equalizing the completion times across jobs and preventing any single link or switch from becoming a bottleneck.

To ensure feasibility, the algorithm rigorously enforces all constraints at each step. The INA placement phase guarantees that exactly K switches are selected and that their processing capacity is respected via the score adjustment mechanism. During the assignment phase, the algorithm maintains the worker exclusivity constraint by ensuring each worker is assigned to exactly one aggregation point—either an INA switch or the PS—by design. The link capacity constraints are checked after every assignment update using the precomputed allPathDict to compute the aggregate load on each edge based on the path incidence indicators and the current rate allocations. If any link exceeds its bandwidth capacity, the algorithm triggers a local re-routing of workers from the most congested paths, favoring paths with fewer hops or lower load. Similarly, the INA switch processing capacity is monitored, and if any switch exceeds its capacity C_s, the algorithm reassigns some of its incoming workers to alternative INA switches or directly to the PS, again guided by a cost function that minimizes the disruption to the overall makespan. The rate consistency constraints (such as gamma_j ≤ gamma_jw for all workers w in job j and gamma_js ≥ gamma_jws) are enforced by setting the egress rate gamma_js to the sum of all incoming rates from workers assigned to that switch, and by ensuring that gamma_j is set to the minimum of the worker rates, thus maintaining the job-level bottleneck property. The use of big-M constraints is avoided by using logical conditions to only activate rates when the corresponding binary assignment is active, and by using zero-padding for inactive workers.

The algorithm is both scalable and robust. Its time complexity is dominated by the K-selection phase (O(|S| log |S|) with sorting and scoring) and the iterative assignment phase (O(I × J × W × S) per iteration, where I is the number of iterations, J is the number of jobs, W is the number of workers, and S is the number of INA switches), which remains tractable even for large networks due to the small value of K (typically a few tens) and the limited number of iterations (usually 3–5). The algorithm is topology-agnostic in design and can be adapted to both FatTree and SpineLeaf architectures by adjusting the path computation and centrality metrics accordingly. It handles edge cases such as jobs with only one worker, small job sizes, or switches with no connected workers gracefully by defaulting to direct PS routing when necessary. While it does not guarantee global optimality, the solution is expected to be near-optimal because it combines greedy selection with iterative refinement and constraint-aware feedback, effectively approximating the optimal trade-off between placement and routing. The use of precomputed paths and efficient load calculation via path-incidence indicators ensures that the algorithm remains practical for real-time deployment in production environments where fast decision-making is critical. By focusing on the most impactful decisions first and iteratively improving the solution through constraint-based feedback, the algorithm strikes a strong balance between performance, feasibility, and computational efficiency.
